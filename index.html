People-pleasing is selfish.

The quote

“Wanting to please other people and wanting to be liked is actually a very self-centred way to live. In seeking approval from others, we focus solely upon our own actions, and less about the community we all exist within”

Reference

The Courage to Be Disliked - Ichiro Kishimi & Fumitake Koga

What I make of it

This idea completely flipped my perspective on people-pleasing. I previously thought that people-pleasing saw people focus too much on other people, and therefore neglect their own needs - how wrong I was. People-pleasing involves focusing on how we appear to other people, and trying to fit a mould of how we think others want us to be, so that we will feel accepted by them. The people pleaser becomes incessantly preoccupied with themselves to the point that it becomes selfish. Instead of people-pleasing, something more fulfilling is to accept ourselves. From this starting point we eliminate much of the need to people-please in the first place, because we no longer seek acceptance from others. Instead of putting ourselves at the centre of our world, we look up, and gaze around at all the other opportunities that life presents us with.

Having to do something to be enough

The idea

If you externalise your self-worth, you’ll never be worthy.

The quote

“If you have to do something or achieve something in order to be someone, then you’ll never be enough. You’ll never unconditionally love yourself, because the condition is that you’re achieving”.

Reference

Where the Light Gets In by Ben Crowe

What I make of it

It sounds cheesy, but it’s true. If your self-worth depends upon you needing to do something in order to win someone’s love or affection, you’re screwed. Why? Because you’ll forever be chasing approval from other people. When you get that approval, life is good. But when you don’t, you’ll be lost in the abyss.

The trade-off of all this is that you end up putting more pressure on yourself to perform, fit in, and be somebody you’re not, (ironically) to get love from someone else. Sounds like a great way to be inauthentic if you ask me.

The antidote

Know that you’re enough as you are. If other people don’t see that, that’s their issue.

March 2026

I hear that things haven’t turned out as you’d planned. The ol’ body is playing up again. How annoying when it doesn’t behave. And now you’ve got tubes coming out of places that weren’t designed for tubes. Your mental state is in the toiletté and you’re feeling about a million bucks worth of Monopoly money. Good times.

As fun as it is to feel sorry for oneself and wallow in a good dose of pity when a life-altering diagnosis waltzes through the door, we can’t do it forever. By all means, I’d advocate for wallowing if that’s what is currently helping. I’ve been there. It certainly scratches an itch.

What I’m interested in talking about is what lies on the other side of wallow: frustration. Frustration and looking up to the ceiling dramatically asking ‘why’. With such frustration comes a unique flavour of insanity. Insanity that spourns from a disbelief and non-acceptance about an assigned diagnoses, and a fear of how what is to come will impact ones life in both the short and long-term. The following article will focus on how to avoid adding insanity to your list of diagnoses after drawing the short end of the health straw.

I’ll start with a little about my own journey so that you’re aware I’m not a total fraud. A while ago I was diagnosed with an aortic valve (one of the heart’s four valves) that has two leaflets instead of three. As a result, a reasonable amount blood would flow back into my heart after each beat, when in fact, it had no business doing so. In order to preserve cardiac function, I underwent open-heart surgery in mid 2025 to replace the shoddy valve. A week in hospital, followed by 4 weeks of cardiac rehab and a graded return to work. All for an otherwise fit and well 30-year-old in the middle of a Melbourne winter. Fun.

As I write this article, I am unwell with an infection of one of the grafted valves. No medical degree is required to know that an infection of your ticker is probably not listed under the Dictionary definition of ‘good’. As it stands, I’ve just finished a week in hospital, with a further 5 weeks of intravenous antibiotics to look forward to. Glorious. It’s always humorous when you become one of the case studies that one reads about. Main character-type energy.

So that’s where I’m coming from. The following is a collection of thoughts and strategies from my own experience that has helped me keep my marbles amongst the health challenges that I’ve had. I am not in a position to provide prescription, given I have no idea what sort of challenges you have faced and how your own unique biopsychsosocial context has shaped how you’ve dealt with those issues. I’m acutely aware that my health issues pale in comparison to many. With that in mind, I hope the following provides one perspective on how not to go insane when you fall ill.

Accept it. Yep, we’re going straight for the psychological jugular with some Buddha material. If you accept the fact that you’re crook, it means your brain can make space for what you can do instead. No longer will you fixate on not running that half-marathon with your mate, but instead you can lock in on some gnarly crochet patterns. I understand completely that this advice is incredibly easy to give and difficult to hede. Skip to the next thing if this isn’t you. I also am aware that acceptance can often feel like rolling over - but it’s actually the opposite. You’re no longer giving power to the turmoil of your illness. Deep, I know. If you fail to accept your health predicament, you will fixate upon it and proceed to run all the possibilities around and around in your head until the marbles fall out. Instead, accept your diagnosis, and move forward.

Focus on what you can control. We’re talking loci here. As mentioned, a consequence of acceptance is your brain then wanting to locate something that it can do something about. Let it. No matter how crummy you’re feeling there’s always something you can channel your energy into. I personally always need a goal or project to work on. Not only does this give you a reason to wake up, but also a purpose, in a time where your usual purpose may have been stripped away from you. For bonus points, pick something that helps others as well as yourself - you’ll be far more satisfied is someone else is benefiting from your struggle.

Use humour. You will literally cry if you don’t laugh. Laugh about how your world has been turned upside down. Laugh about the true insignificance of your life. Joke about the pain and the struggle. Feeling sick absolutely sucks, but what sucks more is not having laughter in your life. Don’t take your illness seriously and it will not consume your mind or your sanity.

Be negative when you need to be. Sometimes, illness will get the better of you. And guess what, that’s completely normal. You’re a human being. For every moment that you are in acceptance, focusing on what you can control, and using humour, there will be another moment where you want to pack it in. An inspirational Michael Jordan video will tell you that you should always turn a negative into a positive, or that you just need ‘a positive mental attitude’. There is a place for these approaches, however sometimes you need to experience the dark in order to cherish the light (this quote sounds like it’s from Star Wars). Letting negativity wash over you will keep you sane because it’ll allow your mind to experience the full spectrum of emotion.

This too shall pass. Again, some more Buddha for you. Like most things in life, eventually, it will pass. The issue may or may not resolve itself, but how you feel about it will. Time will move forward, and so will you. Staying ‘stuck’ with how you’re feeling and believing that you will continue in your current state would drive anyone mad. It will pass.

If you’ve made it this far and think that I’m full of hot wind, I say play on. I’m not you, and I have zero insight into what you’re going through. I genuinely hope that you find clarity and peace in the challenges that you’re experiencing and that you keep some moticum of sanity whilst doing so.

Illness is hard enough without adding psychological insult to injury. Do your best to find the good in life, whilst also riding the wave(s) of challenges that will be thrown your way. I hope that what’s listed above can help you achieve this.

March 2026

Being crook has a strange way of opening your eyes. Illness strips you down to the bare necessities, and in doing so reminds you of what matters in your world. Because you have so little when you’re sick, the things you cling onto are often friends & family, a reason to get up in the morning, and what remains of your health. In turn, one often comes to the realisation that so much of what we do and have in life is ‘extra’. Add-ons that serve little function and that ultimately do not add to the value of our lives. Sure, a new golf putter is nice, but when it comes down to brass tax, it really doesn’t matter too much.

When one returns to normal life following illness, often there is a new appreciation for these essential priorities. It’s liberating to be more cognisant of the things that matter in life. This generates intention in what we do and why we do it. It allows us to say ‘no’ to things that won’t add value. Furthermore, it reduces the energy and time we expend on things that we know are not worth worrying about. And we only have to endure a health crisis to achieve this.

Health, connection, and something to do that gives your life meaning. Illness may reconnect one with these things and hopefully provide an upside to their health challenges.

September 2026

Goals are exciting. They give us direction, focus, and something to channel our energy into. Achieving a goal (hopefully) creates something productive and gives us a sense of accomplishment. From making a ham sandwhich or building a rocket-ship, goals allow us to create the future we want.

Goals are also fundamental to living a life in which our basic needs are met. We all need enough food, water, and shelter (which unfortunately relies upon having enough money) to survive. Without a goal of earning enough money to buy the food our bodies need, it would be difficult to stay afloat.

Goals also serve us more at various stages of life. In the early stages of working, one may have a goal to progress their work and income so as to be able to provide for a future family. After losing fitness, someone may set a goal to get it back. It’s of note that goals tend to reflect what we value, and our desire to attain those things at various points throughout life.

A self-confessed goal-aholic, I love setting a goal and working hard to figure out the best process of attaining that goal. To me there’s something exciting about being able to make something you want, and learn about yourself in the process. Optimisation and efficiency are keys to my approach. What is the most efficient use of my time? Am I applying the 80-20 rule correctly? Am I controlling the variables that are within my locus of control? Juicy stuff.

As I’ve aged, I’ve begun to explore the trade-off of goal-directed focus. Despite goals having served me well, I wonder what other experiences I have missed. I became so good at saying ‘no’ in service of attaining my goals, a good friend once told me I needed to say ‘yes’ more often. Not that I regret any choices I’ve made, and nor will I ever know what the alternative was, but it’s interesting to think about what else life could’ve had for me.

Goals are great, but they’re not exactly famous for providing fertile ground for interesting things to happen. I can’t think of a goal I have set for myself that I’d describe as ‘interesting’. I’ve done interesting things in my life, but rarely have they come as the result of a goal or outcome I have sought - more often they have come about from acting on intuition, feeling or simply doing something a little differently.

In this way, my qualm with goals is that they tend to remove much of the ‘otherness’ of life. In focusing on a particular thing, we create structure and a narrowness to our experience that we forget about what else is out there. When we remove the inefficient and unstructured, we often remove the interesting. And it’s interesting that creates memories, shapes our sense of self and yields more opportunities than most goals ever will.

Goals are excellent at producing outcomes, but outcomes are not necessarily always the goal. Most often in life the gold lies in the journey, or process, along the way. Goal-directed people tend to be outcome focused, but in doing so miss the fun or the learning that comes with the doing. Yes, having a vision and working towards it is fun (and I have certainly experienced this). Though doing things simply for the fun of them can be as rewarding as achieving an arbitrary outcome that we have set.

Goals also create a margin of psychological safety. Having a clear goals gives someone a set of posts to aim for, giving that part of a person’s life structure and safety. We all love and enjoy safety - both of which are key elements of our comfort zone. However if we want to experience ‘other’ removing goal posts removes the margins we have arbitrarily confines ourselves too. When we do so, we might just find interesting.

Not everything in life warrants a goal. Having a goal is useful when applied to the right task, however some things in life just are, and should be enjoyed without a ribbon waiting for us once completed.

Some questions to ponder whether or not a goal is required:

  1. Is there an objective that I need to meet? Reflect upon if the objective is in fact a need or an arbitrary end point you have set
  2. Is there something here I could do a little differently than normal? Acknowledging that it may be inefficient, unknown or less ‘safe’. This also recognises self-imposed limits on your own path.
  3. What’s the most interesting way I could do this?

The key is to discern between what needs a goal and what doesn’t. If you find the things that don’t need a goal, and do them differently, you mind find something interesting. Be. Play. Experience.

An earth-shattering title and a message I’m sure you’ve never been acquainted with. Your body being a temple springs to mind. Despite its cliché message, I certainly believe in it.

The question I ask is, why should health be your top priority? Surely your golf game, family, or paying off your mortgage trump how much sleep you’re getting and how you feel about your waistline. For these people, health is not a priority.

Other people (not me) are currently running half marathons, feeling good in themselves, working hard, and playing sensibly on weekends. Their health poses no current issues - so why then bother with it? If the car is working well, it doesn’t need to be looked at by the mechanic. For these people, health is not currently a problem.

The reason is that you won’t notice how important your health is, until it becomes the only thing that’s important. You might be going along OK at the moment without thinking much about your health, however what commonly happens to young, busy individuals, is that it takes a big health event to stop you in your tracks and make you think. When your Kia breaks down and you can no longer get to work, suddenly it’s a big problem.

I would argue that health is the foundation of a good life. In good health, you are able to grasp every opportunity you wish. You can live in accordance with what you value and have freedom to choose how you spend your time. In poor health, your world becomes small and limited. Having personally experienced both sides of this dichotomy, I can certainly vouch for the former.

Diagnosis of a serious medical condition has the potential to dismantle just about everything you have built in your life. Imagine (and touch wood it doesn’t) that you receive a diagnosis of cancer requiring chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Now imagine trying to work after a round of chemo, when you feel as if you barely have enough energy to stand up and use the bathroom. Such a diagnosis would have a huge impact on your career and earning capacity. Continue to imagine the strain this would cause for your partner and other loved ones. And just as you think it’s done enough, these treatments and chemo have even given your own self-image a serious dressing down. Not to be dramatic, but young people are diagnosed with serious, life-altering health conditions every day. If health is not on your priority list at the moment, at some point in the future when you health goes awry, it will be.

So why not just wait until that fateful day comes? Three reasons. Firstly, paying attention to your health early in life means you can prevent bad things from happening. Secondly, one can identify bad things in their early stages and hopefully treat them before they become bigger issues. Thirdly, you can live healthier. Healthy living means you can do more of the things you love.

Western medicine is much better at reacting to a problem that arises, instead of attempting to prevent it in the first place. Although western medicine is very good at dealing with problems in this fashion, if your goal is to live a healthy lifestyle for as long as possible, prevention needs to be part of your approach.

The antidote to waiting for these things to happen to you, is to be proactive about your health. I know, another incredible insight. Far too many people either wait, and fail to act, or simply allow the business of life to prevent them from seeking help. Have a think about what’s important in your life. Now consider how many of the things you could partake in if you were incapacitated by illness. Probably not many. Early identification of issues, along with getting the basics of health right will go a long way in ensuring you maximise healthspan.

How will humans find meaning once AI takes their jobs?

Work as part of our identity

Since the Industrial Revolution, humans have never really liked working for money, but have had to do it anyway. A societal and economic construct, a person’s job has become a core part of their identity. One big message has been ‘what you do’ rather than ‘who you are’. Most people in developed countries were raised to receive a good education, find employment, and then try their best to stay on HR’s good side so they could continue earning a wage.

Humans also strongly identify with labels. Labelling helps us communicate in a common language and enables us to assign meaning to things. Labelling your mate as a tightass is one such example. Another example is your job title. At parties, we introduce ourselves as our occupation -’ Hi, my name is Barry, and I am a (male escort)’. In this way, we use our job as a way of identifying ourselves to other people. Our job title is used to signal our importance and what we are contributing to society - in Barry’s case, companionship. The label and importance of a job transcends age and stage of life - we ask children ‘what do you want to be when you grow up?’ and ask retirees what they did ‘for a living’. You just hope that none of the kids say they want to like Bonnie Blue when they grow up. I see this labelling all the time in medicine - doctors identifying as a medical specialist first, and person second.

Many people attach so much meaning to the job that they do, I’d argue much of their self-worth and identity are also wrapped up in their job. Barry hopefully takes pride in a job well done, and the identity of someone who is a giver. In this way, people often externalise their self-worth to the name badge they put on in the morning. Historically, this has worked well. A generation ago, people worked for decades at the same organisation, working their way up the hierachy in a linear fashion. The term ‘job stability’ wasn’t even invented (though dare I say for Barry, this won’t be an issue). But things have changed. No longer is there free tertiary education, nor is a job always waiting for the aspiring graduate ready to spread their wings. The Boomers soaked up all the economic propserity, and it’s today’s generation who are drinking the dregs. Some jobs today didn’t exist ten years ago, and some jobs from ten years ago, do not exist today. And all this turbulence before we turn our attention to the elephant in the room: artifical intelligence (AI). Heard of it?

iRobot

It’s no secret that AI will revolutionise the workplace (and holiday recommendations) sooner than we think. AI will not only change the way we work, but what kinds of work we do. Some contend that industries which rely upon objective decision-making (eg. medicine) or protcolised information-based work (eg. accounting) will irreversibly change once governments and companies allow the deployment of AI technologies in these areas. Others proffer that no job or industry is safe. The consensus seems to be that jobs requiring uniquely human skills - humans talking to other humans, emotional intelligence and creative jobs (such as male escorting), will be the least interrupted by AI (though I dare say the algorithm will be able to help men understand how to just listen to their partner and not try and solve anything). Rergardless, it seems that many jobs, in multiple industries, will be outsourced to an algorithm, or at the very least, look nothing like they did before.

Enter the void

If it is true that humans attach much of their self-worth to their job, if that job ceases to exist, it follows that much of their self-worth will erode with it. Attaching your identity to what you do, in an increasingly volatile job market, is becoming somewhat dangerous. Forget shark cage diving, if you have a thing for danger, go and train as a radiologist. The danger is that a psychological and financial hole will be left in a person’s life if their job is taken away from them, and what concerns me, is how people fill that hole. In the past couple of years I’ve had extended periods of time off work due to illness(and no, I didn’t get fired from my job). When I was unable to work, I found there was only so much reading, Netflix-watching and walking I could do to fill the time.

So, excluding the economic challenge that not working would inevitably bring (let’s pray for a universal income), where will people without work find meaning, purpose, and self-worth? Some people can find meaning by playing PS4 all day (I’m not one of them), whilst others are happy to potter around at home. For most people who are not working, I suspect they are likely to feel directionless and will need something to channel their energies into. Subscribing to a Youtube channel unfortunately only gets you so far in terms of purpose. To maintain an identity, it’s almost as if people will have to work on areas in their life outside of work…

People not working in the future will need to learn to reconnect with other things that matter to them (eg. Fin vs. History Podcast). For all the technological developments our world has seen, we will ironically have to retreat to our origins to reconnect with what makes a human being: a sense of community, a healthy mind & body, and the capacity to use our time and energy to make meaningful contributions to society. And if that means re-inventing yourself as an unpaid male escort, then so be it. And if that means you become a mal

With the job market directing itself away from human labour and skill, people who are still currently working may want to consider decoupling their identity from their existing job. By severing their strong attachment to the labels they have been assigned to at work, people may explore what a meaningful life looks like beyond their work alone. Scary stuff for a lot of us, but necessary if you don’t want the algorithm telling you who you should be once you’re not working in a few years.

Back to basics

Strong interpersonal relationships is a fundamental tennet of human biology. Our brains are wired for connection. Basically having mates, a partner to someone (where possible) and a reasonable son/daughter/sibling is critical. If you have none of these things, it might be time to put some work in. Get adopted, read a self-help book about romantic relationships, fire up the Hinge account or just be a better person. Belonging to and contributing to groups in our community can bring immense meaning and worth to a person’s life - so I’d say make this one a priority. Many people have a great sense of belonging with the people that they work with, so it’s worth thinking about how one would fill that need if they were suddenly left without employment and unable to chat with Bill about how your footy team went on the weekend.

Good health also seems important to being human. After being made part of the ‘zipper club’ after two open-heart surgeries, I’m acutely aware of this. For decades, people have become so busy and preoccupied at work, that their health has been compromised. There’s nothing wrong with working hard, and not looking after yourself, though it does seem to catchup with you in the end. The irony of the overworked, overweight doctor has never been lost on me. Some people find purpose in looking after their bodies - you only have to go Instagram to see it. The added benefit is that one feels better and has more energy. If there has ever been a stronger upside to being made redundant, it’s having the time and capacity to be able to do a pushup and drink more green smoothies.

The Japanese philosophy of Ikigai outlines the idea of finding purpose through meaningful work. Ikagi ethos indicates meaningful work is found at the intersection of something you enjoy, something you’re good at and something that contributes to your community. I suspect that in Western society, most people work in a job not because they find it overwhelmingly purposeful, but because they have to in order to keep the air fryer running. It’s also probably the only thing we’ve ever known (taking us back to the question we ask children of ‘what do you want to be when you grow up?’. If the future holds a lot of unemployment, I’m sure there’s room for people to find their Ikigai in unpaid roles. Volunteering at the local RSL, joining a book club, or playing golf for 6 hours a day in order to become a wildlife observer (something I’d personally find Ikigai doing). Even starting a blog (who would ever do that). Either way, we probably need to start looking beyond ‘work’ as we know it in order to find meaning, identity and purpose.

Though much of this article explored the possibility of AI displacing employees from traditional jobs in the near future, the upside of this is AI will be making close to all of your decisions for you in the future - so that’ll make life much easier. The other opportunity it opens is for people to re-define who they are and what they do, aka to have a gap year that runs for the rest of their unemployed life. The power of this cannot be understated. Paradoxically, the technology that shifts us out of the workforce, may actually push us to reacquaint ourselves, with ourselves. Our jobs will no longer be ‘who we are’, but ‘what we did in the good old days’.

For the reasons outline above, I think it’s important for people to develop themselves outside of their job. The idea here is to diversify your investments, so that if one goes through the floor, hopefully the other ones can keep the roof over your head. The future of work is inherently uncertain, though all of us who are currently working can take steps to minimise the psychological and social impact that being displaced from the workforce would have. What won’t AI change?
October 2026

At the moment, there are few more popular buzz terms than artificial intelligence (AI). AI is tipped to be the biggest thing since sliced bread, and of course Britney when she first came on the scene. AI will change the job market, how humans find meaning in their lives, the diagnosis and treatment of previously untreatable medical illnesses, global economic structures, and probably just about every other major industry. It seems as though AI will be the disruptor of disruptors, place a fork in many roads, and likely change the course of human history. Not even Britney did that.

I’d argue, however, that there are some things AI will not be able to change. I don’t think it’s necessarily a good or bad thing; it’s simply an observation. It’s possible that once AI has infiltrated most other aspects of our lives, a select few ‘untouchable’ industries and activities will remain, and that, as a consequence, these things will be highly sought after. Scarcity produces value. In a post-AI world, I imagine humans will crave activities they can engage in without large language models (LLMs), similar to how some communities have chosen to reject shopping at Woollies and Coles, buying only from their local farmers’ market.

The following essay will attempt to identify areas that ‘the algorithm’ will not influence in any demonstrable manner. I will also outline why these areas will exist, and how these activities may change in the future when AI is unable to put its two cents in.

Human love & intimacy

Straight to the soppy stuff. Love and intimacy between humans will not be directly affected by AI. A relief? Perhaps not if you have been using ChatGPT for tips in the bedroom. The fundamental psychological, neurobiological, and emotional processes that love and intimacy are made from will remain unchanged, whether ChatGPT has taken your job or not.

This is certainly a good thing. Intimacy and the connection it brings are fundamental to the human experience, and have been since the birth of modern humans. Despite the vast technological advancements humans have made, we still have brains that share their ancestry with monkeys. We all want to love and be loved, and the only way we can achieve that is through close interpersonal relationships.

Some may argue that AI will be able to teach us how to be a better partner, increase our emotional intelligence, or conduct therapy which could help the individual and/or couple. This may be true; however, it seems unlikely that AI will have a direct influence on a relationship between two humans. My hope is that AI does not become entangled directly in intimate relationships, as if in a love triangle, or that someone feels they have an intimate connection with a particular algorithm (goodness knows this has probably already happened somewhere). Claude is great, but I don’t think he’d be much of a dinner date. That said, for the vast majority, AI really should not intervene in any appreciable way in their intimate relationships.

Human experience - nature & travel

Experiences that cause us to feel awe and inspiration will not be changed by AI. Unless of course you get particularly excited by Claude being able to provide you with the love and reassurance that your Dad never did. Jokes aside, those moments in life where we find ourselves awestruck or amazed will be untouched by an algorithm. In particular, our experiences of nature and travel will remain sacred, and I predict people will return to them to ‘de-program’ themselves once AI begins to run the show.

There is something incredibly unique about the feeling of being in nature and at peace with it. Despite my background in neuroscience, I have absolutely no idea why or how it is different from the connection we can feel to other things, but for those who have experienced it, ‘special’ is the word that comes to mind.

No doubt AI can help with getting out into nature or with planning travel. An algorithm can tell you where the most relaxing coastal walk is, or the cheapest way to fly to Madrid (sorry, travel agents, but you are going to be toast in a couple of years), but it cannot tell you how to feel once you get there. It can’t build suspense or anticipation for seeing your first whale shark on the Ningaloo Reef, nor can it reproduce that feeling of being incredibly small and insignificant lying under a blanket of stars in the night sky. Experiences in nature will be at a premium, providing escape over the coming decades when technology and algorithms begin to dictate many other areas of our lives.

Sport

Footy. I can’t see a world in which AI changes how we play sport, at both amateur and professional levels. Sport is a uniquely human endeavour, and the thrill of watching other humans play a game we are invested in will never die.

Although human-played sport is AI-proof, a very likely scenario is that a robot league will be developed for every sport and code. Robotics has advanced significantly in the past decade, and AI has begun to accelerate it further. One only needs to look at the recent World Humanoid Robot Games in China, where Usain Bolt’s 100 metre world record was smashed by a bot. The commercial application of a robotic sporting league remains to be validated; however, early interest in China, which is at the heart of robotic technological developments, appears strong.

Despite advances in robotics and the AI that powers it, robots will never infiltrate human sport leagues. The long history of humans playing sport, and the fact that humans enjoy watching other humans far too much, will be insurmountable barriers for AI. The pain, ecstasy, disappointment, grit, elation, grief and glory that professional athletes experience and display when they perform for audiences are what make sport as we know it so engaging. That’s the secret sauce - the way sport makes us feel. Robots simply won’t be able to replicate this same emotive experience, even if they optimise other parts of the game itself.

Emotion as a durable currency

The common thread for what artificial intelligence cannot intrude on is emotion. Human emotion. Any activity or pursuit involving the human limbic system will be impregnable to an algorithm. The spontaneity of emotion, the feeling of it, and the way we experience it are so personal, so human, that no set of computer code will ever be able to reproduce them. This speaks to the power of human feeling - seemingly the one thing that an all-powerful algorithm cannot replicate. If we let AI manipulate our emotions, it will. However, we can protect what is uniquely ours and keep seeking these experiences even after AI can tell you where you left your car keys.

If human emotion is the currency of what cannot be influenced by AI, it is vital that we protect it - as it may be the only thing we are able to keep that is truly our own.

Why obstacles in life are often figments of the mind
October 2026

Obstacles are mostly made up. A fugazi, as some Wall Street stockbrokers would say. Our brains trick us into thinking that something is a roadblock when, in truth, it may be a hindrance, a help or neutral.

Your brain believes something is an obstacle after making a value judgement that it is bad. From badness, it makes the extrapolation that it will be inhibitory. Brains do this based on what they have previously encoded to be obstructive (the past) and what they predict to be obstructive (the future). Blame evolution for this. It’s a protective mechanism your brain has developed, designed to prepare or shield you from pain.

Someone wishing to buy a first home may view the minimum deposit required as an obstacle - I certainly did. My brain thought it was a barrier because of the wretched saving I would have to do, the lengthy time that would take, and the costly pleasures (mostly golf) I would have to deny myself in the process. But saving for a deposit is only an obstacle because I made a judgement about it being so. The act of saving money and financing a deposit is actually neither here nor there, neither good nor bad. It simply is. My brain just thought that it was bad.

In reality, very few things are truly obstacles. More often we just think they are obstacles, and either meet them head-on or divert our path so that we need not face them.

I prefer the term ‘opportunity’ over ‘obstacle’. Opportunity is not a label of good or bad. Opportunity encourages one to ask a question, rather than predetermine an answer. ‘We’re all screwed’ becomes ‘what could we try?’. The mind switches from fear to play. From closed to open. Opportunity lends itself to flexible, adaptive thinking. It gives you the opportunity to be curious, instead of fixed and concrete.

If the example above is reframed as an opportunity, suddenly questions are asked and doors are opened. What would the easiest way of saving for a deposit be? Do I have other financing options? Do I need a home ASAP, or is it something that can wait? Could I buy a smaller home first, with a smaller deposit?

Opportunity allows one to reframe not only the thing they thought was a barrier, but also to reconsider the overall objective.

Once one starts reframing their so-called ‘obstacles’ in this way, one realises how much hardship in their life is really just a figment of their psychology. There are objectively difficult things in life; however, I suspect that many so-called ‘issues’ are really just constructed in the mind. Recalibrate these obstacles, and you will find far fewer roadblocks when saving up for that deposit.

When creating something new, the only opinion that matters is yours.

If you think it’s good, it’s good. If you think it’s cool, it’s cool. If you think it’s worth producing, then it’s worth producing. If you think it’s funny, you might want to check with a friend that it actually is funny.

Creators often think if their work is different, it would be better. Your work can always be different - regardless of whether you think it is good. The truth is also that there is no objective metric of what ‘good’ is. Good is what someone thinks of it. This is why the only opinion that truly matters is yours. After all, you’re making art for yourself, not others.

Creators go wrong when they make something that they are happy with but don’t get the same reaction from others. As a result, they sense their work isn’t good enough. This happens particularly during the early stages of their creative/producing journey - perhaps before they’re widely known. If it were the case that every new piece of art, music, or writing needed to be liked by everyone, nothing would ever get produced. It’s fear of other people’s opinions that often stops us.

My partner and I watched Tame Impala play last night. What stood out was when Kevin Parker moved from the main stage to a small stage centred in the standing area and began playing his more techno tracks. These songs weren’t as well known and didn’t get the same response from the crowd. But it’s clear he loved it and thought that the music he’d made was worth listening to. This is the ultimate sign of an artist who creates and performs because they think it’s good.

Think about a professional athlete. During the early stages of their amateur career, before they had made the paid ranks, they had to believe they were good enough to make it to the pros. Maybe they weren’t breaking records as a junior, but they believed they were good enough - and that’s what mattered.

Creators forget that if you think something is good, chances are so does somebody else. For every person whom your work doesn’t resonate with, there’s somebody out there whom it does.

Don’t give those who you think won’t like it a voice - put it out because you’re happy with what you’ve made.

To create wealth, one needs to be a business owner. Owning a business generates income orders of magnitude greater than being an employee. This is because it harnesses the power of other people’s work, not just your own. The wealthiest people on earth own some kind of business. Even those born into wealth - they in effect, become part of ‘the family business’.

Being wealthy doesn’t have to mean owning an entire business. Owning a fraction of a business often suffices. For example, ownership of ETFs is the equivalent of having a stake in a business - albeit a very small one. Being part of a trust is the same. Earning interest from your savings is another example. Regardless of which vehicle is chosen, the principle is the same - wealth comes from your money doing something to earn more money.

Salary is the opposite. You are earning your money through time and effort. The money you earn is not anything to earn more money (unless you direct it to). Those who remain only as employees will always struggle to build true wealth because the scope of their earning potential is limited to their own output.

High-income earners who don’t have ownership in a business mistake their large salary for wealth. These people appear wealthy on paper, however, unless they invest in a business that magnifies their earning potential, they will never be truly wealthy.

Wealth isn’t about how much your salary is. Wealth is about placing your money into a business that harnesses the power of the collective.

You’re the first consumer of your own work. Ground zero. Numero uno.

If you don’t think it’s good, why would anyone else? Make the thing that you want. Make the thing that’s missing, and you can’t find already out there. Build what you think will be impressive or helpful.

Chances are if you’re wanting it, somebody else is too. Make it first for you, and other people might also want it.
